# Humanized: Wisdom Curve article (v3)

## Final version

The design industry rewards velocity. Ship fast, learn the new tool, stay current. Job postings read like tool inventories. YouTube chases whatever launched last Tuesday.

None of that is wrong, exactly. But it creates a blind spot.

Darren Yeo wrote about this in "The Wisdom Curve," using the framework of crystallized vs. fluid intelligence. Fluid intelligence is raw processing speed. It peaks early. Crystallized intelligence is the other kind: pattern recognition built from experience, from every project and platform shift you've lived through. It doesn't peak. It compounds. The industry over-indexes on fluid.

The AI conversation has the same bias. "I built this in an hour" is the flex. Speed again.

Yesterday I had a different experience. I spent the day talking to an AI while building a knowledge system architecture. I wasn't working from requirements docs. I was pulling threads through Burroughs, Joseph Cornell's shadow boxes, Murakami, and a half-remembered Alastair Reynolds novel. Twenty years of reading across disciplines gave me the raw material. What the AI did was let me get it out fast enough to see the connections before I lost them. I've been circling this way of thinking about cross-domain knowledge for most of my career. I just never had the processing capacity to externalize it.

I didn't build something in an hour. I built something that took twenty years. The hour was just when it finally came out.

And what I was building wasn't a prompt for today's flashy UI generator. It was a system that accumulates knowledge to provide creative context to any tool. The tool changes every eighteen months. The knowledge system doesn't.

Yeo ties this to Maslow's self-transcendence, the shift from "what can I get" to "what can I give." I think the practical version of that is mentorship. What I made yesterday wasn't just a system for me. It turned tacit knowledge into something someone else could actually follow. The patterns experience taught me to see, written down in a form where you don't need twenty years to get at them.

That interests me more than the productivity angle. Not "I shipped faster" but "I got something teachable out of my head for the first time."

The article names the real shift: from the innovator's curve to the instructor's curve. Mindset over toolset. The practitioners who've weathered three platform shifts know the tool is temporary. The pattern recognition you built getting through them is permanent.

I keep thinking about all the senior practitioners who've quietly left the industry over the past decade. All that crystallized intelligence, gone. We're still celebrating the fast shippers. Meanwhile AI just handed experienced people the one thing they were missing: a way to finally show what they know. It'd be a shame if nobody used it.
